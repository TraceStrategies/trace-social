Tue, Sep 22Count the handoffs.practice

Drag the image into the post, or find it in the posts folder as 2026-09-22-count-the-handoffs.png

LinkedIn

Pick one process you run every week. Follow a single request from start to finish and count how many times it changes hands.

Every handoff is a place where work sits and waits. Someone has to notice it arrived, understand it, and pick it up. Most delays live there, not in the work itself.

For each handoff, ask one question: does this step add something the customer would pay for? If not, it is a candidate to cut or combine.

Fewer handoffs usually beats faster people.

Forty goals is the same as no goals.

The first step of the TRACE method is Target: pin down the handful of targets that actually decide your year.

Thu, Sep 24Handoffs. Waiting. Rework.faq

Drag the image into the post, or find it in the posts folder as 2026-09-24-three-kinds-of-waste.png

LinkedIn

Lean transformation sounds big. In practice it starts with three kinds of waste you can see this week.

Handoffs: work passing between people or teams, losing context each time.

Waiting: work sitting in a queue, an inbox, or an approval step while nobody touches it.

Rework: work done twice because it was unclear, incomplete, or wrong the first time.

Map how one process really runs, mark every instance of the three, and remove the steps that do not earn their place. The goal is the same output with less friction and lower cost.

Rework: work done twice.

Tue, Sep 29Write the process down before you fix it.practice

Drag the image into the post, or find it in the posts folder as 2026-09-29-write-it-down-first.png

LinkedIn

Before you fix a process, write down how it runs today. Not how it should run. How it actually runs.

Sit with the person who does the work and list every step, every tool, and every decision they make along the way. It usually takes under an hour.

Two things happen. You find steps nobody knew existed. And the fix often becomes obvious once the whole thing is on one page.

A process that only lives in one person's memory cannot be improved, delegated, or covered when they are out.

Most teams do not have a roadmap problem. They have five roadmaps.

Step two of the TRACE method is Roadmap: turn each target into one visualized roadmap across every program.

Put every program on the same timeline and three things show up right away: two teams scheduled on the same people in the same month, work that depends on something nobody scheduled, and dates with no owner.

Sequenced, so everyone knows what comes first. Owned, so every line has a name. On a clock you steer, so dates move because you chose to move them.

Thu, Oct 1Judge a fix by how Monday runs.practice

Drag the image into the post, or find it in the posts folder as 2026-10-01-judge-a-fix-by-monday.png

LinkedIn

A simple way to judge any improvement effort: does Monday run differently?

A report can be right and still change nothing. The recommendation sits in a folder while the team keeps working the old way, because nobody had time to build the new way.

So measure the fix, not the finding. Is the new step in the real system the team uses? Does it run without the person who designed it? Would a new hire follow it without being told?

If the answer is no, it is still advice.

Tue, Oct 6Fix it first. Then automate it.method

Drag the image into the post, or find it in the posts folder as 2026-10-06-fix-then-automate.png

LinkedIn

Automation is step three of the TRACE method, not step one.

Find the manual work quietly eating budget and morale, then hand it off. Only where it is safe. Never on blind trust.

Three checks before automating anything:
1. Is the process already written down and stable?
2. What does one mistake cost if nobody catches it?
3. Who looks at the output, and how often?

If a mistake is expensive, keep a person in the loop. Automating a broken process just breaks it faster.

Wed, Oct 7Name the step a tool removes.faq

Drag the image into the post, or find it in the posts folder as 2026-10-07-name-the-step-it-removes.png

LinkedIn

Before buying any tool, AI included, finish this sentence: "This removes the step where ___."

If you can name the step, you can measure it: how often it happens, how long it takes, and what it costs when it goes wrong. Now you know what the tool is worth.

If you cannot name the step, the tool is a cost looking for a job.

AI is a tool to use where it earns its place. The product is an operation that runs better, not the tool.

Thu, Oct 8Track why work gets redone.practice

Drag the image into the post, or find it in the posts folder as 2026-10-08-track-why-work-is-redone.png

LinkedIn

Rework is the most expensive work a team does. You pay for it twice, and the second time usually costs more.

For two weeks, every time something gets sent back or done over, write one line: what it was and why. Keep the reasons short, like "missing info", "unclear request", or "wrong version".

At the end, sort the list. A few reasons will cover most of it. Fix those at the source, usually with a clearer intake step or a simple checklist, and the rework drops without anyone working harder.

Tue, Oct 13Three questions. Every week. Same time.method

Drag the image into the post, or find it in the posts folder as 2026-10-13-three-question-weekly-review.png

LinkedIn

Step four of the TRACE method is Cadence: a sustainable rhythm that keeps the machine moving and turns hindsight into foresight.

The simplest version is a 30-minute weekly review with the same three questions:
1. What moved?
2. What is stuck, and who can unstick it?
3. What changed that affects the plan?

Same day, same time, same questions. The consistency is what makes problems show up early instead of at the deadline.

Keep it short enough that people can sustain it. Humans included.

Wed, Oct 14Start with one fix, not a program.practice

Drag the image into the post, or find it in the posts folder as 2026-10-14-one-fix-not-a-program.png

LinkedIn

Big transformation programs stall because they ask everyone to change everything at once.

Start with one fix instead. List the problems your team hits, then score each one on two things: how often it happens and how much it hurts when it does. Multiply. The top of the list is your first fix.

Install it, make sure it runs without you, then pick the next one.

The team feels the change in weeks, not months, and each win makes the next one easier to start.

Thu, Oct 15Your team already knows what is broken.practice

Drag the image into the post, or find it in the posts folder as 2026-10-15-ask-the-people-doing-the-work.png

LinkedIn

The fastest diagnostic in any operation is a direct question to the people doing the work: "What slows you down every week?"

They usually know. The problem is rarely knowledge. It is bandwidth. Nobody has time to rebuild a process while also running it every day.

So protect the time. Take the top answer, assign one owner, and give them a few protected hours to fix it. Then ask again.

The people closest to the work are your best source of improvements, if someone gives them room to act.

Tue, Oct 20Every decision: an owner, a date, a reason.method

Drag the image into the post, or find it in the posts folder as 2026-10-20-owner-date-reason.png

LinkedIn

Step five of the TRACE method is Enterprise: govern like the biggest companies do, at your scale. Audit-ready, traceable, investment-planned.

You do not need a compliance department to start. You need one habit: every decision that matters gets written down with three things.

An owner: who made the call.
A date: when it was made.
A reason: why, in one sentence.

Six months later, when someone asks why things work this way, the answer is there. That is what traceable means.

Wed, Oct 21Measure the waiting, not just the work.practice

Drag the image into the post, or find it in the posts folder as 2026-10-21-measure-the-waiting.png

LinkedIn

Pick one request that recently took a week to finish. Now estimate how many hours someone was actually working on it.

For most teams, the hands-on time is a small slice of the total. The rest is waiting: in an inbox, for an approval, for information from another team.

That is why pushing people to work faster rarely shortens delivery. The waiting does not shrink.

Find the longest wait in the process and attack that first. It is usually an approval or a handoff nobody owns.

Thu, Oct 22See the gaps in writing before you act.practice

Drag the image into the post, or find it in the posts folder as 2026-10-22-gaps-in-writing-first.png

LinkedIn

"Things feel slow" is not a problem you can fix. A written list of gaps is.

Before spending money on a fix, walk your operation area by area and write down what is missing: a step with no owner, a process nobody documented, a report nobody reads, a tool nobody uses.

Once the gaps are on paper, patterns show up and priorities sort themselves. The conversation moves from "something is wrong" to "here is what we fix first."

Start at the fix, not at the feeling.
